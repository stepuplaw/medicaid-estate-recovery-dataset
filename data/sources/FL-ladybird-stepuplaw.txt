 What Is a Florida Lady Bird Deed?
 A lady bird deed passes your Florida home to the people you name when you die, without probate. You keep the right to sell, mortgage or change the home for the rest of your life, and nobody named in the deed can stop you. The fee is $399 for one owner and $449 for two owners, such as a married couple, plus the county’s recording charge.
 The formal name is an enhanced life estate deed. The deed splits ownership of the home into two parts. You keep an enhanced life estate for the rest of your life, and a remainder goes to the people you name when you die. You can sell, mortgage, refinance, rent or give away the home, change who inherits, or cancel the deed, all without your beneficiaries’ consent.
 The nickname comes from an old teaching example involving Lady Bird Johnson, and the formal name is the one that goes on the title. Florida has no transfer-on-death deed statute , so the lady bird deed is the main deed-based way to keep a Florida home out of probate.
 Signing the deed transfers no ownership. The transfer happens at death, by operation of law.
 Is There a Lady Bird Law, a Lady Bird Clause or a Lady Bird Trust?
 The instrument is a deed . People search for it under five other names, and the list below says what each name refers to.
 Florida has no lady bird law or lady bird act. No Florida statute creates this deed. The deed rests on common law and the Florida Bar’s Uniform Title Standards 6.10 to 6.12, so several questions about the deed have no controlling Florida decision.
 A lady bird clause is not a separate provision you add to a will. A deed becomes a lady bird deed by reserving an enhanced life estate, meaning the retained power to sell, mortgage or revoke without the remainder beneficiary’s consent. The reservation language is in the deed itself.
 A lady bird trust is not a separate kind of trust. The phrase usually means a lady bird deed whose remainder goes to the trustee of your revocable trust, which is a real and often sensible arrangement. See the Florida lady bird trust .
 A lady bird will is not a document. A lady bird deed does the job people expect a will to do for the house, and the deed controls over the will as to that property. See does a lady bird deed override a will .
 Lady bird title is not a form of title. Title stays in your name while you live, and the deed names who takes it when you die.
 Practice pointer. I never open by correcting the word someone used, because the word tells me what they are worried about. Somebody asking about a lady bird will is thinking about death. Somebody asking about lady bird title is worried about ownership today. The worry decides which document I prepare.
 How Does a Florida Lady Bird Deed Work?
 The deed reserves “enhanced” powers to you, meaning the express right to sell, convey, mortgage, lease, manage, and even completely divest your beneficiaries , all without their signature or consent. The divestment language is the provision that separates this deed from an ordinary life estate. A deed without the divestment language creates an ordinary life estate, which takes away your control and makes a taxable gift.
 An example. Margaret in Naples owns her home and wants her two adult children to inherit it without probate, but she also wants to be free to sell and move to assisted living if she needs to. A lady bird deed lets her keep total control now. If she sells, the children get nothing and have no say. If she still owns the home when she dies, it passes to them automatically, and they clear title by recording her death certificate. Nothing is filed in court, and clearing title usually takes a few weeks.

 Lady Bird Deeds and Medicaid: The Part That Matters Most
 A lady bird deed does not start Florida’s five-year Medicaid look-back, because the state treats the deed as if no transfer happened. The larger benefit comes after death, when the home passes outside probate and Medicaid estate recovery generally cannot reach it.
 The deed is not a penalized transfer. Florida Medicaid has a five-year “look-back.” Give away assets within five years of applying for nursing-home Medicaid and you can be hit with a penalty period. Florida’s Medicaid agency manual states that with a lady bird deed “no transfer has occurred,” because you keep full control and can revoke the deed. Nothing of value has left your hands, so there is nothing to penalize. You could record the deed today and apply for Medicaid tomorrow.
 The deed does not affect eligibility. Your Florida homestead is already an exempt resource for Medicaid (subject to the 2026 home-equity limit of $752,000 where it applies). The deed neither helps nor hurts your ability to qualify.
 The deed generally keeps the home out of estate recovery. After a Medicaid recipient age 55 or older dies, Florida’s estate recovery program tries to recoup what it paid, but only from the probate estate. A home that passes by lady bird deed never enters probate, so the home generally escapes recovery. The deed works best when it is recorded before a health crisis rather than after.
 The mistake to avoid is deeding the house to the children. An outright gift starts the five-year penalty clock, loses the step-up in basis (see below), and cannot be undone. The lady bird deed keeps the home in the family and out of probate, and generally beyond estate recovery, with none of those costs.

 How Does a Lady Bird Deed Work With Homestead?
 If you are married, your spouse must join a lady bird deed on your homestead, or the deed is void as to the home. A lady bird deed also cannot leave the homestead away from a spouse or minor child. Those are the two homestead rules the deed has to satisfy.
 If you’re married, your spouse must join. A married owner cannot convey or mortgage homestead without the spouse joining the deed. A homestead deed that names the children but leaves out a non-joining spouse is void as to the homestead .
 You can’t disinherit a spouse or minor child from homestead. Under Florida law, homestead cannot be left in a will away from a surviving spouse or minor child. A spouse can waive that restriction with a “safe harbor” statement in the deed, but a minor child’s protection cannot be waived. If you have a minor child, a homestead-to-others lady bird deed will fail, and I use a different tool.
 Your tax breaks are preserved during life. Signing the deed is not a change of ownership, so the property appraiser does not reassess. You keep your homestead exemption (roughly $50,000, part of it now inflation-indexed) and your Save Our Homes cap for the rest of your life.
 Tell your family one thing about the exemption. The exemption and the cap do not automatically pass to your beneficiaries. After they inherit, they must apply for their own homestead exemption by March 1 of the next year, or the home gets reassessed at full market value.
 Can You Use a Lady Bird Deed on a Second Home or Rental?
 Yes. A lady bird deed works on any Florida real estate, not just your homestead, and on a rental or vacation property the homestead complications fall away. No spouse has to join the deed, and there is no minor-child restriction, because those rules protect a homestead and not an investment property. The property passes to your family at death outside court, and you keep the rent for life. The step-up in basis still applies, and the step-up often matters more on a long-held rental, which usually carries large built-in gains.
 Two things change. The homestead tax breaks (the homestead exemption and the Save Our Homes cap) never applied to a second property, so there is nothing to preserve. Medicaid also counts a second property differently. Your homestead is an exempt asset for Medicaid. A second property is generally counted like any other real estate, so the property can stand between you and eligibility. A rental that earns market-rate income can sometimes be excluded under Florida’s rules, and a vacation home that sits empty usually is not. A lady bird deed on the second property still avoids probate and generally keeps the property out of estate recovery at death, but the deed does not change how Medicaid counts the property while you are alive. The Medicaid discussion above is about your homestead . If long-term care is likely and you own property beyond your home, the second property needs its own plan, and we work that out at the consult.
 What Does a Valid Florida Lady Bird Deed Require?
 A valid Florida lady bird deed needs six elements. A defect usually comes to light when your family takes the deed to a closing after your death.
 The owner (grantor) correctly identified.
 Enhanced-powers language expressly reserving the right to sell, mortgage, and divest the beneficiaries without their consent.
 Named beneficiaries with survivorship or “per stirpes” backup language.
 The exact legal description copied from your prior recorded deed. The description on the tax bill is often abbreviated and is not a substitute.
 Homestead language and spousal signature where they apply.
 Proper execution and recording. Two witnesses plus a notary, then recorded in the county where the property sits. A deed left in a drawer is missing from the public record your family’s title company searches after your death.
 Snowbirds and out-of-state owners. If you live up north but own a Florida home, the deed still has to meet Florida standards and be recorded in the Florida county where the property sits. Florida allows remote online notarization, so you can sign from home. We handle the recording.
 Tax Consequences: Why This Beats Giving the House Away
 The step-up in basis. Because you keep a life interest, the home stays in your taxable estate, which is the result you want. Inclusion in the estate is what triggers a full step-up in basis to date-of-death value under federal tax law. If you bought for $90,000 and the home is worth $300,000 when you die, your beneficiaries’ basis resets to $300,000. A sale soon after owes little or no capital gains tax.
 A gift works the other way. If you deed the home to the kids during life, they take your old $90,000 basis. A later sale is taxed on the full $210,000 of appreciation, often $30,000 or more in tax (more in the top bracket or with the 3.8% net investment income tax). A gift made during life cannot be reversed afterwards.
 No gift tax and no Form 709. You keep control, so the deed is not a completed gift and there is nothing to report.
 Documentary stamp tax. The Department of Revenue has ruled twice that an enhanced life estate deed transfers no present beneficial interest, so it is not subject to documentary stamp tax regardless of any consideration. One of those two 2020 advisements involved a home with a mortgage on it, and the answer was the same. In practice I record the deed with a nominal recital and a seventy cent stamp. The mortgage-balance rule people repeat here is real, but it belongs to an ordinary deed rather than this one. See Florida documentary stamp tax .
 Estate tax. The home is intentionally in your estate, but Florida has no estate tax. The federal exemption is about $15 million per person for 2026, so the vast majority of Florida homeowners owe none.
 Do Lady Bird Deeds Protect Against Creditors?
 A lady bird deed is not a creditor-protection tool during your life. Liens against you still attach to your interest, and the deed can’t move the home beyond a creditor you already have. The constitutional homestead exemption, not the deed, is what protects your home from most creditors. A judgment lien against you that has never been levied generally does not carry over to your beneficiaries when you die. Your beneficiaries’ own creditors can’t touch the home while you’re alive, because their interest can be wiped out at any time.
 What Happens When the Owner Dies?
 Title vests in your beneficiaries the moment you die, by operation of law. No court, no petition, no new deed. Your beneficiaries clear the public record by recording a certified death certificate and a short survivorship affidavit, usually within a few weeks. The home never enters the probate estate, so a will contest or the probate creditor-claims period, which can hold an estate for months, does not hold up the home.
 While you live, you own and control the home. At your death, title passes to your beneficiaries without probate. Common Lady Bird Deed Mistakes
 Each mistake below can defeat the transfer at death, and the family usually learns of it at a closing after the owner has died.
 Using a generic out-of-state “TOD deed” form. Florida has no transfer-on-death deed statute, so those forms do not work here.
 Leaving out the divestment language. The deed becomes an ordinary life estate, which costs you control and can trigger a taxable gift and a reassessment.
 Married owner signing alone on homestead. The deed is void as to the homestead.
 Naming homestead to others when a minor child is involved. A minor child’s protection cannot be waived.
 No survivorship language. A beneficiary who dies first sends their share to probate.
 Never recording the deed. The deed has to be recorded in the county where the home sits, and your family cannot rely on a deed missing from the public record.
 A later refinance or reverse mortgage overriding the deed. The same powers that make the deed flexible mean a later loan or conveyance can defeat the death transfer. Before any refinance, HELOC, or reverse mortgage, and again at death, I confirm the lady bird deed is still operative.

 Frequently Asked Questions
 Why Is It Called a Lady Bird Deed?
 The name is a piece of law school folklore rather than Florida law. The technique is usually credited to a Florida professor who used a hypothetical in which President Lyndon Johnson conveyed land to his wife, Lady Bird, and the nickname stuck to the instrument. No Florida statute uses the term, and nothing about the deed traces to the Johnsons themselves. The formal name is an enhanced life estate deed, and that is the name you will see on a properly drafted instrument and in the Florida Bar’s Uniform Title Standards.

 Does a Florida lady bird deed avoid probate?
 Yes. When the owner dies, title passes automatically to the named remainder beneficiaries outside probate, as long as the deed is properly drafted and actually recorded. The beneficiaries record a certified death certificate (and usually a short survivorship affidavit) to clear title.

 Can I sell or refinance the home after signing?
 Yes. The “enhanced” powers let you sell, mortgage, refinance, lease, or give the property away during your lifetime without the beneficiaries’ consent. If the home is your homestead and you are married, your spouse still has to join the deed for a sale or mortgage.

 Can I change or cancel the deed later?
 Yes. The right to change or revoke is what makes the life estate “enhanced.” You revoke or change beneficiaries by recording a new deed. No one’s consent is required.

 Will a lady bird deed trigger gift tax or a Form 709?
 No. Because you keep full control and can revoke it, signing the deed is not a completed gift. There is no federal gift tax and no Form 709, and you don’t use any of your annual exclusion or lifetime credit.

 Does it help me qualify for Medicaid?
 No. Your Florida homestead is already exempt for Medicaid eligibility, so the deed neither helps nor hurts you there. The deed matters at death. The home passes outside probate, and Florida’s Medicaid estate recovery claim runs against the probate estate, so the claim generally cannot reach the home.

 Does a lady bird deed cause a Medicaid transfer penalty?
 No, when it is drafted as a true enhanced life estate deed. Florida’s Medicaid agency manual (DCF ESS §1640.0613.01) states that with a lady bird deed “no transfer has occurred,” so it does not start the five-year look-back penalty. A plain gift of the home does trigger the penalty.

 What about capital gains taxes?
 A lady bird deed preserves the step-up in basis at death (IRC §1014). The beneficiaries’ basis resets to the home’s date-of-death value, so a sale soon after owes little or no capital gains tax. Giving the home away during life instead loses the step-up and can cost the family tens of thousands in tax.

 Does my spouse have to sign?
 If the property is your Florida homestead and you are married, then yes, your spouse must join the deed (Art. X §4(c)). To leave the homestead to someone other than your spouse, the spouse also signs a §732.7025 waiver of homestead rights.

 What if I have a mortgage?
 You can still use a lady bird deed, and recording it does not by itself trigger a due-on-sale clause. The Florida Department of Revenue ruled in 2020 that an enhanced life estate deed on a mortgaged home is not subject to documentary stamp tax, because the deed transfers no present beneficial interest.

 What if a beneficiary dies before I do?
 The deed should include survivorship or “per stirpes” language so a beneficiary’s share passes to the surviving beneficiaries or their descendants. Without it, a deceased beneficiary’s share can be forced into probate. The simple fix during life is to record a new deed.

 I own property in another state, can a lady bird deed help?
 A Florida lady bird deed only works on Florida real estate. For out-of-state property, a revocable living trust is usually the better tool to avoid a second (ancillary) probate.

 Is a lady bird deed the same as a transfer-on-death deed?
 No. Florida does not have a transfer-on-death deed statute, so generic TOD-deed forms from other states do not work here. The enhanced life estate (lady bird) deed is Florida’s deed-based way to pass real estate at death without probate.

 Will a lady bird deed change my property taxes or homestead exemption?
 Not while you are alive. Signing the deed is not a change of ownership, so the appraiser does not reassess the home, and you keep your $50,000 homestead exemption and the Save Our Homes cap that limits assessment increases to 3% a year. At your death, the exemption and the cap do not carry over. The beneficiary who moves in must apply for their own homestead exemption with the county property appraiser by March 1 of the year after they inherit, or the home is reassessed at full market value. The same reset happens on any transfer of the home.

 Does a lady bird deed protect my home from my creditors?
 No. While you are alive you keep full ownership and control, so a creditor’s judgment lien still attaches to the home and has to be cleared before you can sell. The constitutional homestead protection is what shields your Florida home. A judgment lien against you that is never enforced during your life generally does not follow the home to your beneficiaries after you die.

 Can my beneficiary’s creditors, divorce, or bankruptcy reach the home?
 While you are alive, no. Your beneficiary’s interest can be erased at any time because you can still sell or change the deed, so their creditors cannot touch it. After you die, a home left outright to a beneficiary becomes their asset and is exposed to their creditors, a divorce, or bankruptcy. If a beneficiary carries that risk, the fix is to leave the home to a continuing trust instead of to the person directly, so the protection lasts past your death.

 Can I name my living trust or more than one person as the beneficiary?
 Yes. You can name several beneficiaries, and you can name your revocable living trust so the home flows into a continuing sub-trust when you die. The hybrid keeps the deed’s lifetime tax and Medicaid advantages while routing the home into a protective trust, whether a special needs trust for a beneficiary on Medicaid or SSI or a spendthrift trust for one exposed to creditors or divorce. Leaving the home straight to a person fits a single, solvent adult who will live there.

 What Seven Florida Cases Show About Lady Bird Deeds
 In 14 years of law practice, I have handled the disputes that follow a signed deed. I also litigate deeds after they fail, which enhances the practice of drafting deeds.
 Five Florida appellate opinions use the term “lady bird deed” or “enhanced life estate deed.” Two more, decided in 1917 and 1942, construe the reserved power that makes the deed operate. I have analyzed all seven in depth, and I see flawed deeds recorded on a daily basis.
 Clients are often confused about whether the deed costs them their homestead exemption, and ask me, "What wording preserves the exemption?" The honest answer is that no wording does it, because signing the deed is not a change of ownership, so the property appraiser has nothing to reassess and the exemption and the Save Our Homes cap simply continue. The wording that matters is the reserved power, and it is doing a different job.
 In reviewing these cases, I have a few take-home points.
 The first is control. I draft these so that an owner can prevent a future beneficiary from taking control of the owner’s property during the owner’s lifetime, and that retained control is the purpose of the instrument. I have reviewed a case going all the way back to 1917 where a father deeded land to his daughter, kept the right to resell it, and then sold it out from under her. She went to court to save her interest and she lost, because the deed said in plain words that he could do exactly that. Cases with this shape keep coming up, and it is usually a grown child who assumed the house was already theirs. Avoid any version of this deed that leaves out the reserved power, because that reservation is what preserves your control.
 Second, the difference between an enhanced life estate deed and an ordinary life estate deed comes down to one provision. In a recent case I reviewed, a widow went to sell the marital home after her husband died and could not do it, because their deed gave the two of them a life estate and never authorized a sale. She tried to work around it using her position as trustee of the trust that held half the remainder, and that failed as well. I see this all the time. The couple’s deed named the right people and divided the remainder exactly the way they wanted, and the family still could not sell the house. Avoid form deeds, because a reader of the document does not notice a provision that was never written.
 Third, a deed prepared by someone who benefits from it is not automatically void, and this is the point that surprises the families who call me. I have come across a case where an attorney drafted a deed that moved the remainder to himself and his wife, and even that did not void it on its face. The drafting shifted the burden of proof, which is not the same thing. Somebody in the family still has to file suit and prove what happened, and by then the owner has usually died and cannot explain any of it. Avoid assuming that a questionable deed corrects itself.
 Lastly, almost none of these disputes were really about the law. I see the same three failures over and over, which are a missing provision, a deed nobody recorded, and a name typed into the wrong blank. Every one of them was preventable at the drafting stage for a few hundred dollars, and I would rather spend that time with you now than have your family spend years on it later.
 Florida has no statute creating this deed, which rests instead on common law and the Florida Bar’s Uniform Title Standards. Several questions therefore have no controlling Florida decision, and I will identify those when they apply to your property rather than offer an answer the cases do not support.
