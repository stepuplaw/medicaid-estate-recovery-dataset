# Findings

Counts, conflicts with the popular secondary lists, and the findings most likely to surprise a reader. All 51 rows were verified on September 23, 2026. The Florida row was rechecked before publication against the Florida statute text (Secs. 409.9101, 733.707, 736.05053, 736.1109, 689.15 and ch. 711). That review corrected the wording of the hardship waiver criteria and added the Sec. 409.9101(6) survivor bar and the Sec. 409.9101(10) sale rule. No classification changed.

## 3. Conflicts with the popular lists

### medicaidlongtermcare.org scope table
Updated Jan 30, 2026, saved as `data/sources/US-popular-mltc-estate-recovery.txt`; run `tools/compare_popular.py` to reproduce. The page as read today names 23 probate-only states plus DC and 27 expanded states. The earlier "says 27, names 23" problem noted in the evidence report is no longer present. The page also notes its chart "has not been updated for 2026". Conflicts:
- **Alabama:** listed expanded; ours probate_only. State Plan Att. 4.17-A item (7) defines the estate by Alabama probate law, and no expansion statute or rule was found.
- **Arizona:** listed expanded; ours probate_only. The ALTCS rule R9-28-901 defines the estate by the probate code (A.R.S. 14-1201), and the AHCCCS brochure says claims go only against probate or small estate property. Karp 2005 also had Arizona as probate-only.
- **Arkansas:** listed as "expanded, but only a home with a Lady Bird Deed"; ours probate_only. Act 570 of 2021 removed the beneficiary deed claim, and the DHS manual says no recovery from a recorded beneficiary deed.
- **Mississippi:** listed expanded; ours probate_only. Miss. Code 43-13-317 recovers through the probate claim statute, and In re Estate of Darby (Miss. Ct. App. 2011) held the definition is not expanded.
- **Colorado:** listed probate_only; ours expanded_partial. C.R.S. 15-15-407(4) makes beneficiary deed property expressly subject to HCPF recovery.
- **Connecticut and Maine:** listed expanded; ours expanded_partial. This is a nuance, not a reversal: Connecticut reaches only annuities beyond probate, and Maine excludes joint tenancy real estate.
- **Other secondary claims contradicted by primary text:**
  - Pages calling Georgia, Virginia or Nebraska probate-only are wrong: the rules or statute expressly reach non-probate assets.
  - A claim that Oregon "confirmed" a caregiver-child home exception in 2025 legislation is wrong: SB 1029 (2025) died in committee.

### TOD deed lists
Nolo's list, saved as `US-popular-nolo-tod.txt`, has 34 jurisdictions. It matches this dataset exactly except that it omits Maryland, effective 2026-10-01. Pages giving 29, "about 30" or 31 are out of date: they miss some of Georgia (2024-07-01), New Hampshire (2024-07-01), New York (2024-07-19), Delaware (2025-12-04) and Maryland (2026-10-01). "33 plus DC" is correct as of today but will be one short on Oct 1, 2026. Several claims found during research are false:
- **Kentucky:** pages cite a "KRS 394B (2020)" TOD statute. LRC records show the bills never passed through 2026.
- **Idaho:** pages cite a TOD law effective July 2026. S1399 died in committee.
- **South Carolina:** one site says a lady bird bill was "recently enacted". H. 4264 is still in House Judiciary.

### The "five lady bird states" list (FL, TX, MI, VT, WV)
Source: ElderLawAnswers (Jul 2023) and Nolo, saved as `US-popular-ela-ladybird.txt` and `US-popular-nolo-tod.txt`.
- **West Virginia:** no primary authority found. No statute, reported case or Medicaid rule recognizes a deed reserving a power to sell without the remainder's consent. The nearest statute, W. Va. Code 36-1-16, addresses powers given to another person, not powers the grantor keeps.
- **Texas:** recognized by the HHSC Medicaid handbook (I-3100). No Texas statute creates the deed; Estates Code ch. 114 is the TOD deed act.
- **Missing from the list:**
  - Rhode Island (statute, 2014)
  - Maryland (case law)
  - North Carolina (Medicaid manual)
  - Virginia (general power-of-disposal statute)

## 4. Ten findings most likely to surprise a reader

1. **In seven probate-only jurisdictions a TOD deed is reachable but a plain life estate is not.** AK, DC, HI, IL, MO, NM and NY all limit recovery to the probate estate. But their TOD acts (or Missouri's nonprobate transfer law, Sec. 461.300) make the beneficiary liable for estate claims when the probate estate is short. In all seven the traditional life estate is `not_reachable`, and the lady bird deed is `unclear` because no primary authority recognizes it there. No state has a TOD deed that is reachable alongside a lady bird deed confirmed not reachable. In the 10 states where the lady bird deed is `not_reachable`, the TOD deed is not reachable (TX, WV), unclear (CA, OK) or does not exist (FL, LA, MI, NC, RI, VT).
2. **A revocable living trust is reachable in 13 of the 27 probate-only jurisdictions:** AL, AK, AZ, DC, HI, IL, MA, MO, NC, SC, TN, VT and WV. The route is the trust code rule (UTC Sec. 505(a)(3) and equivalents) that makes a revocable trust liable for the settlor's debts when the probate estate is insufficient. Florida does the same for a non-homestead house (Sec. 733.707(3)); a protected homestead is excluded. A living trust is generally a worse shield than a lady bird deed.
3. **Some TOD deed acts shield the house expressly, while others single out Medicaid.**
   - **Shield the house:**
     - Texas: Estates Code 114.106(b) excludes TOD property from MERP.
     - Arkansas: Act 570 of 2021 repealed the beneficiary deed claim.
     - West Virginia and Mississippi: both adopted TOD acts without the uniform creditor-liability section.
   - **Single out Medicaid:**
     - Nebraska requires every TOD deed to carry a warning that the beneficiary is personally liable for Medicaid reimbursement (Sec. 76-3418).
     - Wyoming title does not pass until the health department certifies there is no Medicaid claim (W.S. 2-18-103(n)).
     - Kansas (59-3504(b)), South Dakota (29A-6-421), Minnesota (507.071 subd. 3) and Nevada (111.693) name Medicaid recovery in the TOD act itself.
4. **Three of Karp's 13 probate-only states now go after non-probate property.** Nebraska, New Hampshire (2005) and Ohio (2005) now expressly reach joint tenancy, life estates and trusts. Nebraska and New Hampshire now have TOD deed acts that make the beneficiary liable.
5. **Georgia expands recovery by agency rule alone, and a 2026 statute may cut against it.** The statute does not define the estate. DCH rule 111-3-8-.02(7) and the Sept 2025 DCH FAQ reach joint tenancy, life estates, trusts and TOD deeds. HB 413 (signed 2026-04-22) now says a Georgia TOD deed "takes precedence and priority over all unsecured debts" of the estate. That conflicts with the FAQ, and no court has resolved it.
6. **California is probate-only by statute, but its regulation was never updated.** SB 833 limits the estate to probate assets for deaths from 2017. The unamended regulation, 22 CCR 50961, still describes reaching revocable remainders and spouse estates. California's TOD deed, originally a pilot, now runs to 2032 (Prob. Code 5600(c)), and its creditor sections (5672 to 5677) leave it `unclear`.
7. **Iowa may be the harshest state for the family home.** Iowa Code 249A.53(2)(c) reaches jointly held property, retained life estates and trusts. Recovery also runs against the surviving spouse's estate. Iowa Code 561.19 denies homestead protection against the Medicaid debt even when the home passes to children.
8. **Rhode Island has a lady bird statute but penalizes using it.** R.I. Gen. Laws 34-4-2.1 has authorized the deed since 2014, yet under Sec. 40-8-3.1 a lady bird deed made on or after July 1, 2014 makes the home a countable resource for Medicaid eligibility. Vermont's 2020 Enhanced Life Estate Deed Act is the cleanest lady bird statute: its Medicaid rule HBEE 29.08 imposes no transfer penalty, and recovery is probate-only. In Vermont, though, a house in a revocable trust is reachable.
9. **Liens can reach a house even where the estate cannot.**
   - **Oklahoma:** probate-only, but its homestead lien (63 O.S. 5051.3) survives a TOD transfer and severs a joint tenancy.
   - **Maryland:** imposes TEFRA liens on the homes of permanently institutionalized recipients (probate-only, COMAR 10.09.24.15A(4)). Its new TOD act (effective 2026-10-01) has no creditor section, so the first Maryland TOD deeds are `unclear` if a lien is in place.
   - **Delaware:** the new act (effective 2025-12-04), by contrast, makes TOD property liable for estate claims (Sec. 215).
10. **Statute and agency text conflict in several states, sometimes on life estates.**
    - **New Jersey:** the statute lists "life estate", but the rule (N.J.A.C. 10:49-14.1) excludes one that ends at death, so a traditional life estate escapes.
    - **North Dakota:** the Supreme Court (Krueger, 2019) says the full expanded definition applies, while the HHS manual says life estates are "not generally subject" to recovery.
    - **Indiana:** the statute reaches joint and trust property; the Medicaid manual says they are not in the estate.
    - **Pennsylvania:** the DHS rule says revocable trust assets are not subject to the claim; the trust code (20 Pa.C.S. 7745) says they are liable when the probate estate is short.
    - **Mississippi:** the same conflict between Medicaid rule and trust code (Sec. 91-8-504).
